ARTICLE:
Minimum due is the smallest amount your credit card company asks you to pay by the due date, so your account stays in good standing and you avoid a late fee. It is usually a small slice of the total bill, and whatever you leave unpaid keeps collecting interest.

In short: paying the minimum keeps you out of trouble for this month. Paying the total due keeps you out of interest.

That is why many people who pay only the minimum, month after month, feel the bill never shrinks. Most of each small payment goes to interest, not to the money they actually spent.

## A simple ₹ example

Say your card bill is ₹20,000 and the minimum due is 5% of it: ₹1,000. You pay ₹1,000 and leave ₹19,000 unpaid. At an example interest rate of 3% a month (rates differ by card, so check your own statement), next month's interest is about ₹570 on that ₹19,000.

So after paying ₹1,000, the balance has only come down by about ₹430 in real terms, and any new spending is added on top. Our free Minimum Due Calculator [link: https://moneyviel.com/money-tools/minimum-due-calculator/] shows how many months or years this can take with your own numbers.

## The common mix-up

Minimum due is not the same as total due. The statement shows both. The minimum due is what you must pay to avoid a late fee; the total due is the full amount you owe for that cycle. Paying the full total due by the due date is what usually avoids interest on purchases.

Another mix-up: thinking the minimum is a 'plan'. It is only a floor. A fixed amount that you choose, and that is higher than the minimum, clears the bill much faster.

## Where you'll see it

- On your monthly credit card statement, next to 'Total amount due' and 'Payment due date'.

- In your card's app or SMS alerts as 'Min. amount due'.

- On some pay-later and store-card bills, which can work in a similar way.

If you have more than one debt, the Debt Payoff Planner [link: https://moneyviel.com/money-tools/debt-payoff-planner/] compares two simple ways to clear them, and our Money Words [link: https://moneyviel.com/money-tools/money-words/] page explains 100+ other money terms in simple English.

## Your small step today

Open your last card statement and write down two numbers: the minimum due and the total due. If you can, set your payment to the total due, or at least a fixed amount above the minimum.

Verdict: Myth. Prices usually go up a little every year. This is called inflation, and it means the same ₹100 note tends to buy a bit less as time passes.

A note does not lose its number, but it slowly loses its buying power. Over a year you may hardly notice. Over ten or twenty years, the difference is large, which is why planning in future rupees matters.

## A ₹ example

Let's assume prices rise 6% a year (an assumption for this example, not a forecast). A ₹15 cup of tea would cost about ₹27 in 10 years and about ₹48 in 20 years.

Looked at the other way: ₹100 kept as cash for 10 years would buy only about ₹56 worth of today's things. After 20 years, about ₹31 worth. Try your own numbers in the free ₹ Time Machine (inflation calculator) [link: https://moneyviel.com/money-tools/inflation-calculator/].

## Why people believe it

Day to day, prices seem steady, and a ₹100 note looks exactly the same as it did last year. The change is slow and spread out, so it is easy to miss until we compare prices from many years apart.

## Where inflation shows up in daily life

You see it in school fees, rent, groceries, a doctor's visit or a wedding budget. These costs rarely go down over the years. A salary that stays the same for several years can feel smaller, even though the number on the payslip has not changed.

It also matters for money kept aside for many years. If savings grow slower than prices, the money can buy less in the future, even though the amount looks bigger on paper. This is why money for different goals is usually planned differently: short-term money for safety, long-term money with time to grow.

## What to do instead

- Plan big goals in future rupees. A goal 10 years away will likely cost more than today's price. The free Life Goals Planner [link: https://moneyviel.com/money-tools/goal-planner/] does this maths for you.

- Keep emergency money safe and easy to reach. Short-term money is about safety, not growth.

- Learn how long-term money can grow. Long-term goals usually need growth that beats inflation. Returns are never guaranteed, so learn the basics first.

To make a budget on a ₹25,000 salary, start with your essentials (rent, food, bills, travel), then save a fixed amount on salary day before you spend, and give the rest a clear limit for wants. Write it down once and check it every week.

You don't need an app or a spreadsheet. A notebook and four lines are enough: Needs, Savings, Wants and a small Buffer for surprises.

## Step 1: Know your real take-home pay

Use the amount that actually reaches your bank account each month, not the salary on your offer letter. If your income changes month to month, use your lowest normal month.

## Step 2: List your essentials

Essentials are the costs you must pay to live and work: rent, groceries, electricity and phone bills, travel to work, any EMIs, and money you send home. Look at last month's bank and UPI history so the numbers are real, not guesses.

## Step 3: A ₹25,000 example plan

Here is one example for a single person living in a shared flat. Your numbers will be different; the method is what matters.

- Needs: ₹14,000 (rent share ₹6,500, groceries ₹4,000, bills ₹1,500, travel ₹2,000)

- Savings, paid first: ₹3,500 (moved on salary day; that is ₹42,000 in a year)

- Wants: ₹4,000 (eating out, outings, shopping)

- Family / health: ₹2,000

- Buffer: ₹1,500 (small surprises, so you don't dip into savings)

Total: ₹14,000 + ₹3,500 + ₹4,000 + ₹2,000 + ₹1,500 = ₹25,000. Every rupee has a job.

You may have heard of the 50/30/20 idea (50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings). On ₹25,000 that is ₹12,500, ₹7,500 and ₹5,000. It's a useful starting point, but if rent is high in your city, adjust it. A plan you can follow beats a perfect plan you can't.

## Step 4: Make it run on autopilot

- On salary day, move your savings amount to a separate account first.

- Pay rent and bills in the first few days.

- Keep your wants money in one place and stop when it runs out.

- Every Sunday, spend 5 minutes checking what's left.

- At month-end, move any leftover to savings or your emergency fund.

## Common budgeting mistakes to avoid

- Saving whatever is left. Usually nothing is left. Save first, on salary day.

- Forgetting yearly costs. Festivals, insurance renewals, school fees and birthdays come once a year. Divide them by 12 and set that amount aside every month.

- Making the plan too strict. If wants get ₹0, the plan breaks in a week. Keep a small, fixed amount for fun.

- Not counting small UPI payments. ₹30 here and ₹80 there can add up to thousands a month. Check your UPI history weekly.

- Giving up after one bad month. Every budget needs a few months to settle. Adjust and keep going.

## Step 5: Review every month

If you overspent on wants, don't give up; just set a slightly more realistic limit next month. If you have money left over, slowly raise your savings. Your first big savings goal is usually an emergency fund of 3 to 6 months of essentials.

## Quick answers

How much should I save from a ₹25,000 salary?
Start with what you can keep up every month, even ₹1,000–₹2,000, and raise it slowly. Around 10–20% is a common target once essentials are covered.

What if my rent takes half my salary?
Keep needs as low as you can, cut wants first, and still save a small fixed amount. The habit matters more than the size at first.

Should I use cash or UPI for my budget?
Either works. What matters is a clear limit for each line and a weekly check.
